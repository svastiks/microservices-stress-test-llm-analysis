\begin{abstract}
Cloud microservices are often deployed with excess CPU, memory, and replica count to avoid SLO violations under peak load.
We study \textit{bidirectional} right-sizing: scaling \textbf{down} from an over-provisioned baseline until the first SLO failure (upper-bound / cost-effective frontier), and scaling \textbf{up} from an under-provisioned baseline until recovery (lower-bound / minimum viable provisioning).
We compare two decision engines on the same closed loop (k6 load, Prometheus telemetry, Kubernetes apply): \textbf{Method~1}, a deterministic formula controller, and \textbf{Method~2}, a large language model (LLM) that reads the same structured experiment record and emits full Deployment/HPA YAML.
On Robot Shop \texttt{web} at 25\,RPS (DOWN), the LLM arm reaches a lower-cost passing configuration in fewer iterations than the formula arm (e.g.\ cost score 0.22 vs.\ 0.37 at the last PASS before \texttt{first\_fail}).
At 220\,RPS (UP recovery from a thin 50\,mCPU / 25\,MiB pod), the LLM arm recovers in three iterations at cost 0.14 vs.\ formula's four iterations at cost 0.18.
Formula sizing is computed entirely in Python; the LLM is not invoked for YAML during formula runs.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
Operating microservice fleets is expensive when capacity is chosen conservatively.
Horizontal Pod Autoscaler (HPA) reacts to utilization but does not, by itself, answer: \textit{what is the cheapest Deployment and HPA configuration that still meets SLO at a fixed target load?}

We frame this as \textbf{efficiency mapping} along two directions:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{DOWN (over-provisioned start):} begin with generous resources; iteratively reduce CPU, memory, and replicas while SLOs pass; stop at \textbf{first fail}. The last PASS is the \textit{upper-bound / cost-effective} configuration for that workload intensity.
    \item \textbf{UP (under-provisioned start):} begin with intentionally thin resources; iteratively increase capacity while SLOs fail; stop at \textbf{first pass}. The first PASS is the \textit{lower-bound / recovery} configuration.
\end{itemize}

Both directions share the same observables---achieved RPS, error rate, $p_{95}$ latency, CPU and memory utilization vs.\ requests---and the same objective \texttt{cost\_score} (replica-weighted sum of CPU and memory \textit{requests}).
The research question is whether an LLM can match or beat a transparent formula policy on \textbf{iterations to the boundary} and \textbf{cost at the boundary}, without coupling the two arms at decision time.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}
\textbf{Kubernetes autoscaling.} HPA and VPA adjust replicas or resources from utilization signals; they optimize reactivity, not a fixed-workload cost frontier.
\textbf{Performance testing and SLO-driven tuning.} Load generators (e.g.\ k6) plus observability backends are standard; our contribution is the \textit{closed-loop} policy that proposes the \textit{next} YAML from telemetry, not a one-shot recommendation.
\textbf{ML/LLM for operations.} Recent work uses LLMs for incident triage and config suggestions; we isolate a controlled compare: same cluster protocol, same JSON schema, two optimizers.
\textbf{Baselines.} Method~1 (formula) is our primary baseline; HPA-at-fixed-target is the implicit production comparator the squeeze loop is meant to improve upon.

\section{Methodology}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{System architecture}
The pipeline (\autoref{fig:placeholder}) is:
(1)~apply workload via k6 constant-arrival-rate profile;
(2)~collect Prometheus metrics into \texttt{experiment.json};
(3)~compute SLO PASS/FAIL and \texttt{cost\_score};
(4)~run the optimizer (formula \textit{or} LLM);
(5)~write \texttt{recommended.diff} via \texttt{difflib} against repo YAML, apply to cluster, settle, repeat.

\subsection{Method 1: Formula controller}
\textbf{No LLM} is called for sizing when \texttt{squeeze\_optimizer=formula}.
Each iteration:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Python computes \texttt{scaling\_hint} $\in \{\mathrm{UP},\mathrm{DOWN},\mathrm{HOLD}\}$ from SLO status, utilization, and squeeze mode.
    \item \texttt{step\_pct} is computed from observed pressure (Section~\ref{sec:unified-step}).
    \item \texttt{\_maybe\_apply\_deterministic\_efficiency\_yaml} scales requests/limits (and HPA bounds on DOWN) on the on-disk Deployment/HPA files.
    \item \texttt{recommended.diff} is the unified diff of old vs.\ new YAML (pure Python).
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Method 2: LLM controller}
\textbf{LLM} receives the same \texttt{experiment.json} fields plus current YAML text.
It returns JSON with \texttt{deployment\_yaml\_new}, \texttt{hpa\_yaml\_new}, and a short report.
Post-processing enforces safety (e.g.\ veto scale-down on FAIL+UP, replica one-step clamps on DOWN, metric-based UP recovery guards when memory is saturated or $p_{95}$ is near SLO).
\textit{Design principle:} compare arms do not read each other's outputs; the LLM is not given the formula's numeric recommendation as an oracle (early prototypes that floored LLM steps to formula steps were removed as unfair).

\subsection{Objective and cost}
\begin{equation}
    \texttt{cost\_score} = N_{\mathrm{eff}} (w_1 R_{\mathrm{cpu}} + w_2 R_{\mathrm{mem}})
\end{equation}
with $R_{\mathrm{cpu}}, R_{\mathrm{mem}}$ in millicores and MiB requests, $N_{\mathrm{eff}}$ effective replicas.
SLO: $p_{95} \le 500$\,ms, error rate $\le 1\%$.

\subsection{Unified fractional step (formula)}
\label{sec:unified-step}
\medskip
\noindent One bounded \texttt{step\_pct} per iteration scales vertical resources:
\begin{align}
    \text{DOWN:}\quad & x' = (1-\texttt{step\_pct})\,x, \\
    \text{UP:}\quad & x' = (1+\texttt{step\_pct})\,x.
\end{align}

\medskip\noindent\textbf{DOWN (PASS):}
\begin{align}
    \texttt{slack} &= \max\!\left(0,\frac{60-\max(u_{cpu},u_{mem})}{60}\right), \nonumber\\
    \texttt{step\_pct} &= \texttt{bound}(b + 0.25\,\texttt{slack},\, f,\, 0.30),
\end{align}
\noindent with $(b,f)=(0.10,0.10)$ or $(0.05,0.05)$ if utilization $\ge 60\%$.

\medskip\noindent\textbf{UP (FAIL):}
\begin{align}
    \sigma &= \max\bigl(\tfrac{err}{err_{SLO}}-1,\,
        \tfrac{p95}{p95_{SLO}}-1,\nonumber\\
    &\quad\,\tfrac{rps_{target}}{rps_{achieved}}-1\bigr), \\
    \texttt{step\_pct} &= \texttt{bound}(0.15 + 0.08\min(\sigma,3),\, 0.15,\, 0.40).
\end{align}
\noindent HPA replica step:
$\Delta=\max(1,\lceil maxReplicas\cdot\texttt{step\_pct}/2\rceil)$.

\subsection{Compare protocol (formula vs.\ LLM)}
Each showcase run executes \textbf{two sequential arms} on the cluster:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Reset to profile baseline (DOWN: managed web baseline; UP: thin 1\,pod, 50\,mCPU, 25\,MiB, HPA max\,=\,1).
    \item Run full squeeze with \texttt{optimizer=formula} $\rightarrow$ \texttt{squeeze-compare-formula/}.
    \item Reset baseline again.
    \item Run full squeeze with \texttt{optimizer=llm} $\rightarrow$ \texttt{squeeze-compare-llm/}.
    \item Emit \texttt{comparison.md} aligned by iteration index.
\end{enumerate}
Both arms share \texttt{SQUEEZE\_MAX\_ITERATIONS=16} on UP; DOWN LLM uses \texttt{SQUEEZE\_LLM\_PURE=1} (no deterministic YAML fallback).

\subsection{Decision policy sketch (from notes)}
\needspace{6\baselineskip}
\noindent\footnotesize Pseudocode (boundary-seeking squeeze):
\begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{1pt}\setlength{\parskip}{0pt}
    \item \texttt{obs} $\leftarrow$ Prometheus + k6; \texttt{fail} $\leftarrow$ ($p_{95}\!>\!$SLO) $\lor$ (err) $\lor$ (cpu\_util $>\! 95\%$).
    \item \texttt{DOWN}: fail $\Rightarrow$ STOP (boundary $\leftarrow$ prev PASS); else scale DOWN.
    \item \texttt{UP}: $\neg$fail $\Rightarrow$ STOP (boundary $\leftarrow$ PASS); else scale UP.
    \item M1: \texttt{step\_pct} $\leftarrow f_{\mathrm{formula}}$; M2: YAML $\leftarrow$ LLM + guards; apply(diff).
\end{itemize}

\noindent\textbf{Notes vision (future).}
Exponential steps, explicit bounds, error-aware termination, and few-shot exemplars remain future work; the deployed system uses conservative fractional steps and HOLD gating.

\section{Experimental Setup}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Service:} Robot Shop \texttt{web}, namespace \texttt{svastik}, login workload (\texttt{POST /api/user/login}).
    \item \textbf{down\_demo:} 25\,RPS, 90\,s, SLO $p_{95}\le 500$\,ms; stop at \texttt{first\_fail}.
    \item \textbf{up\_demo:} 220\,RPS, 90\,s, same SLO; thin baseline then UP recovery until \texttt{recovered\_from\_underprovisioning}.
    \item \textbf{up\_demo\_strict:} 260\,RPS (ongoing sweeps).
    \item \textbf{Telemetry:} Prometheus CPU/memory utilization vs.\ requests; k6 $p_{95}$ and achieved RPS.
    \item \textbf{Settle:} post-rollout delay before each iteration k6 run.
\end{itemize}

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}
We highlight curated \texttt{showcase\_DOWN} and \texttt{showcase\_UP} compares.
Iteration rows are \textit{not} simultaneous A/B tests---formula runs first, then LLM on a fresh reset---so row-aligned $p_{95}$ differences include run-to-run variance.

\noindent\textbf{Findings.}
The LLM arm consistently reaches a \textbf{lower-cost} last PASS with \textbf{fewer iterations} (e.g.\ run-34: 7 vs.\ 11 iterations; $\approx$40\% lower cost score).
Both arms monotonically reduced CPU/memory requests while utilization rose---the joint signal of real slack consumption, not monotonic $p_{95}$ (latency can fall as caches warm).
Pure-LLM DOWN uses prompt + replica clamps only; formula uses deterministic DOWN steps only.

\noindent\textbf{Primary showcase (run-44).}
Iter.\,1--2: both FAIL on thin 50/25 (severe $p_{95}$).
Iter.\,3: formula still FAIL at 98/49\,m ($p_{95}\!\approx\!1024$\,ms); LLM PASS at 94/47\,m ($p_{95}\!\approx\!423$\,ms).
Iter.\,4: formula PASS at 121/61\,m.
LLM wins on \textbf{iteration count} and \textbf{cost at PASS} with a single replica throughout.

\noindent\textbf{Interpretation caveat.}
At iter.\,2 both arms used 70/35\,m requests but LLM saw much lower $p_{95}$ (1633 vs.\ 3674\,ms) before the arms diverged---sequential runs, not locked-step coupling.
The comparison table shows \textit{requests}, not limits; LLM PASS configs often set higher limits relative to requests.
Metric guards (memory saturation, near-SLO cap) act on the LLM arm only and are derived from \texttt{experiment.json}, not from formula output.

\subsection{What we measured vs.\ open questions}
\textbf{Measured:} iterations to boundary, \texttt{cost\_score} at boundary, utilization trajectory, replica count.
\textbf{Open (from notes):} reward/penalty training loop; providing formula as \textit{hint} vs.\ independent LLM; exponential steps; explicit upper/lower bound bracketing; error-rate-terminated vs.\ latency-only FAIL.

\section{Discussion}
\textbf{When formula wins.} UP runs with tied iterations can still favor formula on cost if the LLM overshoots steps (showcase run-43, pre-guard fix).
\textbf{When LLM wins.} DOWN frontier and run-44 UP show fewer steps and lower provisioned cost at SLO.
\textbf{Fairness.} Formula \texttt{recommended.diff} is always Python-generated; LLM diff is LLM YAML after guards---neither arm depends on the other for per-iteration numbers.

\section{Conclusion}
We presented a bidirectional stress-test loop for microservice right-sizing and compared a deterministic formula controller to an LLM controller on equal telemetry and workloads.
On Robot Shop demos, the LLM arm finds cheaper passing configurations in fewer iterations on both DOWN (25\,RPS) and UP recovery (220\,RPS), while the formula arm remains a reproducible, auditable baseline.
Future work includes stricter UP loads (260+ RPS), multi-run variance bands, policies from your notes (exponential steps, explicit bounds, error-aware termination), and ablations on how much formula guidance may be given to the LLM without invalidating the comparison.
